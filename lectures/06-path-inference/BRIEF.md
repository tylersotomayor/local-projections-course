## 1. Session brief

**Opening situation.**
A referee is reading two sentences. The first is from Inoue, Jordà, and Kuersteiner's shelter-inflation application (their Figure 4): "the response of shelter PCEPI is almost uniformly negative each month over the 4 years displayed", written under a figure whose pointwise Newey–West bands contain zero at every one of the 49 months. The second is from Jordà and Taylor's unemployment application (their Figure 6a): the unemployment rate "gradually increases to about 1¼ percent approximately two years after impact, and returns back to zero about four years after impact", written under a figure whose pointwise bands exclude zero at 13 of 49 months and whose joint test rejects "no response at any horizon" with $\chi^2(49)=446.9$. The referee's question is the lecture's question: which of these sentences does the evidence support, and which procedure would have to be run to know? The spine's shorter version opens the notes: "the response is negative throughout the first year" is one sentence about five quarterly horizons (thirteen monthly ones), and a pointwise band was built to make one statement at a time.

**Decision or empirical question.**
Given a stacked vector of estimates $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\hat\beta_0,\dots,\hat\beta_H)'$ and a horizon set $\mathcal H$ named by a claim, which null hypothesis does the claim state, which statistic tests it, and what does the corresponding band or test promise in repeated samples? Six claims recur: (i) "nonzero at month $h$" (pointwise interval); (ii) "negative at every month in $\mathcal H$" (simultaneous band or one-sided joint test); (iii) "different from zero somewhere in $\mathcal H$" (Wald test); (iv) "lower on average over $\mathcal H$" (one linear combination); (v) "the cumulative effect through $h$ is $B_h$" (accumulated response, a linear combination with the full covariance); (vi) "curve A differs from curve B" (a direct test with the cross-curve covariance). Every one of them needs the off-diagonal elements of $\boldsymbol\Sigma$, the object Lecture 5 ignored.

**Target student and prerequisites.**
The student has completed Lecture 5 (coverage, Newey–West, the score $s_t u_{t,h}$, lag augmentation), Lecture 2 (cumulative response $B_h$, common sample $\mathcal T_H$), Lecture 3 (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell partialling out), and Lecture 4 (LP-IV, `gmm` seen once). Mathematical prerequisites: variance of a linear combination $\boldsymbol\ell'\boldsymbol\Sigma\boldsymbol\ell$, the multivariate normal, and the $\chi^2$ distribution of a quadratic form; the bivariate normal is used once in the scalar case and stated when needed. Stata prerequisites: `regress`, `newey`, `predict, resid`, a `forvalues` loop over horizons, matrices via `matrix` and a short Mata block that the lab project supplies.

**Learning outcomes (five).**
1. Translate a sentence about a response path into a null hypothesis over a named horizon set $\mathcal H$ and choose the procedure that tests it: a pointwise interval, a simultaneous band, a Wald test, or a single linear combination.
2. Estimate the cross-horizon covariance $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ of $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ by partialling out the controls, stacking the horizon-specific scores $\tilde s_t\tilde u_{t,h}$, and applying one HAC estimator to the stack; verify the construction against Stata's system `gmm` with `vce(hac nw m)` to numerical precision.
3. Compute pointwise, Bonferroni, and sup-$t$ bands from $(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta},\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma})$, explain why $c_{1-\alpha}\ge z_{1-\alpha/2}$ and why the sup-$t$ band is narrower than Bonferroni whenever horizons are correlated, and state what each band's coverage promises.
4. Compute the standard error of any linear combination of horizons — an accumulated response, an average over a window, a difference between two curves — from the full covariance, and show numerically what the sum-of-variances shortcut and the overlapping-bands heuristic get wrong.
5. Read Inoue–Jordà–Kuersteiner significance bands as a test under the null of no response at all, distinguish them from confidence bands, and say which of the two the referee's sentence needs.

**Anchor numerical and data examples.**

*A. The known economy (IJK Figure 3 design).* `Figure3.do` from the Econometrics-Journal repository (commit `5e57e0b`, 29 Nov 2024; `Replication Code/Figure3.do`). Seed 12345; 1,000 burn-in observations then $T=150$; innovations $u_{y,t},u_{x,t}\sim N(0,1)$ i.i.d. The comment in the file calls the design a bivariate VAR(1) with $a_{yy}=a_{xx}=0.7$, $a_{yx}=a_{xy}=0.4$, $b_{yx}=1$. **As coded it is not.** The two `replace` lines run sequentially, so when `y` is generated `x` is still identically zero: the data-generating process is
$$y_t=0.7\,y_{t-1}+u_{x,t}+u_{y,t},\qquad x_t=0.4\,y_{t-1}+0.7\,x_{t-1}+u_{x,t},$$
a triangular system with both eigenvalues 0.7 (the VAR described in the comment has eigenvalues 1.1 and 0.3 and would explode). Verified in `scratchpad/design-06/ijk/dgp_check.do`: regressing the simulated $y_t$ on $y_{t-1},x_{t-1}$ gives 0.014 (s.e. 0.068) on $x_{t-1}$. The LP in the file regresses $x_{t+h}$ on $y_t$, $y_{t-1}$, $x_{t-1}$ for $h=0,\dots,13$, so the intervention is $s_t=y_t$, the outcome is $x$, the controls are $\mathbf w_t=(y_{t-1},x_{t-1})'$, and the population LP coefficient is
$$\beta_h=\frac{[\mathbf A^h\boldsymbol\Sigma_v]_{x,y}}{[\boldsymbol\Sigma_v]_{y,y}}=0.7^{\,h-1}\,(0.4h+0.35),\qquad \mathbf A=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0\\0.4&0.7\end{pmatrix},\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_v=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},$$
i.e. $0.5,\,0.75,\,0.805,\,0.7595,\,0.6688,\,0.5642,\,0.4622,\,0.3706,\,0.2924,\,0.2277,\,0.1755,\,0.1342,\,0.1018,\,0.0768$ for $h=0,\dots,13$ (`handcalc3.py`, matrix and closed form agree to $10^{-16}$). Units: response of $x$ per unit of the reduced-form innovation in $y$ (the regression coefficient on $y_t$ given the lags). This is the economy the lab simulates and the coverage exercise reuses; the notes disclose the sequential-`replace` fact in the evidence section, because the truth the students compare against must be the truth of the code they ran.

*D. Two-horizon hand economy.* The AR(1) outcome of Lecture 1, $y_t=\rho y_{t-1}+\theta_0 s_t+v_t$, $s_t\sim$ i.i.d.$(0,\sigma_s^2)$, $v_t\sim$ i.i.d.$(0,\sigma_v^2)$, LPs at $h=0$ and $h=1$ with control $y_{t-1}$ on the same rows. Derived: $\operatorname{Corr}(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1)\to\rho/\sqrt{2+\rho^2}$ when $\theta_0=\sigma_s=\sigma_v=1$; Monte Carlo check with $T=200$, 4,000 replications, seed 20260913 (`handcalc3.py`): 0.314 versus 0.333 at $\rho=0.5$; 0.527 versus 0.537 at $\rho=0.9$; probability that both pointwise 95% intervals cover: 0.886 (bivariate-normal prediction 0.906) and 0.899 (0.910).

**Smallest useful model.**
Two horizons of the Lecture 1 economy on a common sample. The horizon-$h$ regressions are $y_{t}=\mu_0+\beta_0 s_t+\gamma_0 y_{t-1}+u_{t,0}$ and $y_{t+1}=\mu_1+\beta_1 s_t+\gamma_1 y_{t-1}+u_{t,1}$ with $u_{t,0}=v_t$ and $u_{t,1}=\theta_0 s_{t+1}+v_{t+1}+\rho v_t$. Because $s_t$ is unpredictable, $\sqrt T(\hat\beta_h-\beta_h)\approx T^{-1/2}\sum_t s_t u_{t,h}/\sigma_s^2$, so
$$\boldsymbol\Sigma\approx\frac{1}{T\sigma_s^4}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_s^2\sigma_v^2 & \rho\,\sigma_s^2\sigma_v^2\\ \rho\,\sigma_s^2\sigma_v^2 & \sigma_s^2\{\theta_0^2\sigma_s^2+\sigma_v^2(1+\rho^2)\}\end{pmatrix},$$
and the two estimates are correlated through the shared $v_t$ even though each score is serially uncorrelated (Lecture 5's point). With $r=\operatorname{Corr}(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1)$, the probability that two pointwise 95% intervals both cover is $P(|Z_0|\le1.96,|Z_1|\le1.96)$ for a standard bivariate normal with correlation $r$: 0.9025 at $r=0$, 0.9182 at $r=0.733$ (the Figure 3 design's $(0,1)$ correlation), 0.95 only at $r=1$. The sup-$t$ critical value is the $c$ that makes this probability 0.95: 2.2365 at $r=0$ (equal to Šidák), 2.1719 at $r=0.733$, 1.96 at $r=1$; Bonferroni uses 2.2414 regardless. Everything in the general case is this picture with $H+1$ coordinates.

**Dependency chain of sections** (spine order preserved; titles refined; the scalar case lives inside section 2).

1. `#sec-l06-three-claims` *Three claims about one curve.* The referee's sentences are nulls over a horizon set $\mathcal H$; "negative at month 6", "negative at every month in $\mathcal H$", and "different somewhere in $\mathcal H$" are three hypotheses, and a table matches each to the procedure that tests it and to the object the procedure needs.
2. `#sec-l06-joint-distribution` *The joint distribution of the stacked estimates.* Two horizons of the Lecture 1 economy show that $\hat\beta_0$ and $\hat\beta_1$ share $v_t$ and are correlated; the general statement $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\approx N(\boldsymbol\beta,\boldsymbol\Sigma)$ follows by stacking the scores $\tilde s_t\tilde u_{t,h}$ after partialling out the controls, and $\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}=\hat{\mathbf S}/(T\hat q^2)$ with one Bartlett HAC estimator on the stack is the same matrix Stata's system `gmm` reports with `vce(hac nw m)`.
3. `#sec-l06-simultaneous-bands` *Pointwise versus simultaneous coverage.* The probability that all $|\mathcal H|$ pointwise intervals cover falls from 0.95 toward $0.95^{|\mathcal H|}$ as correlation weakens (0.51 for 13 independent horizons); Bonferroni restores family-wise coverage by dividing $\alpha$, and the sup-$t$ band restores it with the smallest constant $c_{1-\alpha}$ that the estimated $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ allows, computed by the three-step plug-in algorithm.
4. `#sec-l06-joint-tests` *Joint tests over a horizon set.* The Wald statistic $W_{\mathcal H}=\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\mathcal H}'\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_{\mathcal H}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\mathcal H}\sim\chi^2_{|\mathcal H|}$ answers "different somewhere" and nothing about where; with many highly correlated horizons it is also numerically fragile, and a single linear combination $\boldsymbol\ell'\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ — an average over the window — is the interpretable alternative.
5. `#sec-l06-curve-differences` *Differences between curves.* Two responses to the same intervention have covariance $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{YX}$, so $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta^Y_h-\hat\beta^X_h)=\Sigma^{YY}_{hh}+\Sigma^{XX}_{hh}-2\Sigma^{YX}_{hh}$; overlapping bands and star-counting ignore the third term and are not tests.
6. `#sec-l06-accumulated` *Accumulated responses.* $\operatorname{Var}(\hat B_h)=\boldsymbol\iota'\boldsymbol\Sigma_{0:h}\boldsymbol\iota$ needs every covariance below the diagonal, the long-difference LP delivers the same number in one regression on a common sample, and a cumulative multiplier inherits the whole matrix.
7. `#sec-l06-significance-bands` *Significance bands.* Under the null of no response at any horizon the estimator's variance simplifies to $\sigma^{0\,2}_h=g_h/\gamma_{sz}^2$ with $g_h$ the long-run variance of $z_ty_{t+h}$, so a band around zero can be drawn from an auxiliary regression of $\eta_{t,h}=z_ty_{t+h}$ on a constant; it answers "is there any response?", not "how precise is $\hat\beta_h$?".
8. `#sec-l06-evidence` *Evidence: a known economy and two published paths.* IJK Figure 3 (Newey–West versus sup-$t$ in the known economy), the shelter path (three bands, one Wald test, one sentence), and the unemployment path (13 pointwise stars, zero sup-$t$ exclusions, a rejected Wald, and an average over months 12–36 that is 2.35 standard errors from zero).
9. `#sec-l06-summary` *What the lecture built and what it leaves open.* One covariance matrix answers every claim about the path; the next lecture asks why two estimators of the same path can disagree in a sample.

**Likely glossary terms** (owned keys plus two new keys; definition drafts).
- `cross-horizon-covariance` — The off-diagonal part of the covariance matrix $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ of the stacked LP estimates: how the sampling error of $\hat\beta_h$ moves with that of $\hat\beta_k$. It is nonzero because the horizon-$h$ and horizon-$k$ regressions share the same intervention dates and overlapping future shocks, and it is what every claim about more than one horizon needs.
- `stacked-regression` — The $H+1$ horizon-specific local projections written as one system, estimated jointly (by system GMM or by stacking the partialled-out scores) so that one covariance matrix for all horizons can be computed. Coefficients equal the equation-by-equation OLS estimates on a common sample; only the covariance is new.
- `pointwise-band` — The set of horizon-by-horizon confidence intervals $\hat\beta_h\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\hat\sigma_h$. Each interval covers its own $\beta_h$ with probability $1-\alpha$; the band as a whole covers the path with a lower, unreported probability.
- `simultaneous-band` — A band constructed so that the probability that it contains $\beta_h$ at every $h$ in $\mathcal H$ at once is at least $1-\alpha$. It supports sentences about the whole path; its width is the price of that support.
- `sup-t-band` — The simultaneous band $\hat\beta_h\pm c_{1-\alpha}\hat\sigma_h$ whose constant $c_{1-\alpha}$ is the $1-\alpha$ quantile of $\max_h|Z_h|/\sigma_h$ for $\mathbf Z\sim N(\mathbf 0,\boldsymbol\Sigma)$ (Montiel Olea and Plagborg-Møller 2019). It is the narrowest band of this shape with simultaneous coverage $1-\alpha$, and it is never narrower than the pointwise band.
- `bonferroni-band` — The simultaneous band that uses $z_{1-\alpha/(2|\mathcal H|)}$ in place of $z_{1-\alpha/2}$. It guarantees family-wise coverage without knowing the correlations and pays for the ignorance: it is wider than sup-$t$ whenever horizons are correlated.
- `multiplicity` — The fact that a claim about several horizons is several hypotheses at once, so that per-hypothesis error rates compound. Thirteen independent 95% intervals cover jointly only 51% of the time.
- `family-wise-error-rate` — The probability that at least one hypothesis in a family is falsely rejected, or that at least one interval in a band misses. Simultaneous bands and joint tests control it; pointwise bands control only the per-horizon rate.
- `joint-hypothesis` — A null that restricts several response coefficients at once, $\mathbf R_{\mathcal H}\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf 0$. "No response during the first year" is a joint hypothesis with $|\mathcal H|$ restrictions; rejecting it says the coefficients are not all zero and does not say which one is not.
- `wald-test` — The test that compares $W_{\mathcal H}=\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\mathcal H}'\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_{\mathcal H}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\mathcal H}$ with a $\chi^2_{|\mathcal H|}$ critical value. It weights the estimates by their inverse covariance, so it can reject strongly while every pointwise interval contains zero, and it becomes numerically fragile when $\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_{\mathcal H}$ is nearly singular.
- `curve-difference-test` — A test of $\beta^Y_h=\beta^X_h$ (at one horizon or over $\mathcal H$) that uses the covariance between the two curves' estimates. Whether the two pointwise bands overlap is not this test: overlap ignores the covariance and uses the wrong critical value.
- `accumulated-response-uncertainty` — The standard error of $\hat B_h=\sum_{j\le h}\hat\beta_j$, $\sqrt{\boldsymbol\iota'\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_{0:h}\boldsymbol\iota}$, which includes every covariance among the summed horizons. The sum of variances alone understates it when responses are positively correlated, which they are.
- `significance-band` — A band around zero, $\pm z\,\hat\sigma^0_h$, whose width is computed under the null that the intervention has no effect at any horizon (Inoue, Jordà, and Kuersteiner 2026). A response that leaves it is evidence of some response; the band says nothing about the precision of the estimate that left it.
- `horizon-set` (new key) — The set $\mathcal H$ of horizons a claim names, with $|\mathcal H|$ its size. Writing $\mathcal H$ down before testing is what makes a claim testable; "throughout the first year" at monthly frequency is $\mathcal H=\{0,\dots,12\}$.
- `linear-combination-test` (new key) — A test of one weighted sum $\boldsymbol\ell'\boldsymbol\beta$, such as the average response over a window, with standard error $\sqrt{\boldsymbol\ell'\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\boldsymbol\ell}$. It answers a narrower question than a Wald test, with one degree of freedom and an interpretable sign.

## 6. Derivations to verify

**D1. Two-horizon covariance (`eq-l06-two-horizon-cov`).** Source: the Lecture 1 economy $y_t=\rho y_{t-1}+\theta_0s_t+v_t$ with $s_t$ i.i.d. and independent of $v$. Steps: (i) substitute forward once: $y_{t+1}=\rho^2y_{t-1}+\rho\theta_0s_t+\theta_0s_{t+1}+v_{t+1}+\rho v_t$, so $\beta_1=\rho\theta_0$ and $u_{t,1}=\theta_0s_{t+1}+v_{t+1}+\rho v_t$; (ii) with $y_{t-1}$ as the only control and $s_t$ unpredictable, $\hat\beta_h-\beta_h\approx(T\sigma_s^2)^{-1}\sum_ts_tu_{t,h}$; (iii) take the covariance of the two sums: $\mathbb E[s_t^2u_{t,0}u_{t,1}]=\sigma_s^2\rho\sigma_v^2$, cross-$t$ terms vanish because each contains an odd power of an independent mean-zero $s$; (iv) $\operatorname{Var}(s_tu_{t,1})=\sigma_s^2(\theta_0^2\sigma_s^2+\sigma_v^2(1+\rho^2))$; (v) divide. Result: $\operatorname{Corr}(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1)=\rho\sigma_v^2/\sqrt{\sigma_v^2(\theta_0^2\sigma_s^2+\sigma_v^2(1+\rho^2))}$, $=\rho/\sqrt{2+\rho^2}$ at unit parameters. Numerical check: `handcalc3.py`, $T=200$, 4,000 replications, seed 20260913: $\rho=0.5$: MC correlation 0.314 (theory 0.333), $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_0)$ 0.00536 (theory $1/199=0.00503$), $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_1)$ 0.01148 (0.01131), Cov 0.00247 (0.00251); $\rho=0.9$: 0.527 (0.537), 0.00524, 0.01402 (0.01412), 0.00452 (0.00452). Report both, with the finite-sample gap named.

**D5. Accumulated response and the long-difference equivalence (`eq-l06-accumulated`).** Source: $\hat B_h=\boldsymbol\iota'\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\Delta}_{0:h}$ where $\hat\beta^{\Delta}_j$ are period-change LPs ($\Delta y_{t+j}$ on $s_t,\mathbf w_t$) on the common sample; $\operatorname{Var}(\hat B_h)=\boldsymbol\iota'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{\Delta}_{0:h}\boldsymbol\iota=\sum_j\Sigma^\Delta_{jj}+2\sum_{j<k}\Sigma^\Delta_{jk}$. Because $y_{t+h}-y_{t-1}=\sum_{j=0}^h\Delta y_{t+j}$ and OLS is linear in the dependent variable, the long-difference LP coefficient equals $\hat B_h$ exactly on identical rows and regressors (Lecture 2), and its score is $\tilde s_t\sum_j\hat{\tilde u}^{\Delta}_{t,j}$, so its HAC variance equals $\boldsymbol\iota'\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^\Delta\boldsymbol\iota$ exactly with the same bandwidth. Numerical check to run in Stata (`supt_shelter4.do`): $\max_h|\hat B_h-\hat\beta^{LD}_h|$ and $\max_h|\operatorname{se}(\hat B_h)-\operatorname{se}(\hat\beta^{LD}_h)|$ both below $10^{-10}$; and the off-diagonal share of $\operatorname{Var}(\hat B_h)$ at $h=3,12,24,48$. «S4-ACC»

**D6. Difference of two curves (`eq-l06-curve-difference`).** Source: stack both outcomes' scores, $\mathbf g_t=(\tilde s_t\tilde u^{Y}_{t,0},\dots,\tilde s_t\tilde u^{Y}_{t,H},\tilde s_t\tilde u^{X}_{t,0},\dots)'$; one Bartlett HAC gives the $2(H+1)$-square matrix whose blocks are $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{YY},\boldsymbol\Sigma^{XX},\boldsymbol\Sigma^{YX}$; $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta^Y_h-\hat\beta^X_h)=\Sigma^{YY}_{hh}+\Sigma^{XX}_{hh}-2\Sigma^{YX}_{hh}$. The overlap heuristic declares "different" only if $|\hat\beta^Y_h-\hat\beta^X_h|>1.96(\hat\sigma^Y_h+\hat\sigma^X_h)$, which is the right test only when $\Sigma^{YX}_{hh}=-\hat\sigma^Y_h\hat\sigma^X_h$ and $1.96(\hat\sigma^Y+\hat\sigma^X)$ replaces $1.96\sqrt{\hat\sigma^{Y2}+\hat\sigma^{X2}}$: with equal variances and zero covariance it is $\sqrt2$ too strict; with positive covariance it errs in both directions. Hand check (`handcalc.py`): $\operatorname{Var}^Y=\operatorname{Var}^X=0.25$: Cov $=0$ gives s.e. 0.707 (overlap threshold 1.96 vs the correct 1.386); Cov $=0.20$ gives s.e. 0.316 (correct threshold 0.620). Data check (`supt_shelter4.do`): tenant rent (`pcerent_tenant`) versus owners' equivalent rent (`pcerent_owner`), both as $100\log$, same shock, same controls, common sample; report the correlation $\operatorname{Corr}(\hat\beta^{\mathrm{ten}}_h,\hat\beta^{\mathrm{own}}_h)$, the difference and its $t$ at $h=0,3,6,12,18,24,30,36,42,48$, the count of months with $|t|>1.96$ whose bands overlap, the reverse count, and $W_{\{12,24,36\}}$. «S4-DIFF»

**D7. Significance band (`eq-l06-sigband`).** Source: IJK eq. (16)–(19), just-identified IV with instrument $z_t$ ($z_t=s_t$ in the shelter case), $n=T-h$. Steps: (i) $\sqrt n(\hat\beta_h-\beta_h)=n^{-1/2}\sum_tz_tu_{t,h}/(n^{-1}\sum_tz_ts_t)$; (ii) under $H_0:\beta_h=0$ the numerator is $n^{-1/2}\sum_tz_ty_{t+h}$ (after partialling out), so its long-run variance $g_h$ can be estimated from $\eta_{t,h}=z_ty_{t+h}$ without using $\hat\beta_h$ — the LM principle; (iii) the denominator converges to $\gamma_{sz}$; (iv) $\sigma^{0\,2}_h=g_h/\gamma_{sz}^2$ and the band is $\pm z_{1-\alpha/(2(H+1))}\sqrt{\hat g_h/n}/\hat\gamma_{sz}$, the Bonferroni factor because the band is read at $H+1$ horizons at once; (v) special case $z=s=y$ white noise: $g=\gamma_y^2$, $\gamma_{sz}=\gamma_y$, $\sigma^0=1$, the $\pm1.96/\sqrt n$ autocorrelogram band. Implementation (authors' `Figure4.do` lines "Do Significance bands as in the paper"): `newey eta_h, lag(48)` gives $\sqrt{\hat g_h/n}$ as `_se[_cons]`; divide by the mean of $\tilde s_t^2$. Numerical check (`shelter_papertext.log`): band half-widths 0.190 ($h=0$), 1.514 (12), 3.354 (24), 4.978 (36), 5.952 (48) against $|\hat\beta_h|$ of 0.066, 0.225, 1.502, 2.679, 3.618: the response never leaves the band under the course specification, whereas the joint Wald rejects at $10^{-7}$ over the first year. The as-shipped `Figure4.do` (outcome log completions, smoothed) gives $\hat\beta_{42}=-30.7$ against a band of 46.5 and an $F(49,370)=10.41$ joint test; 752 s runtime with the wild bootstrap (`run_fig4a.log`).

**D8. sup-$t$ plug-in algorithm and its Monte Carlo error (`eq-l06-supt`).** Source: MOPM (2019) Theorem/algorithm as restated in IJK §5 and JT §7.2: $P(\boldsymbol\beta\in\hat{\mathbf B}(c))\to P(\max_h|V_h|/\sigma_h\le c)$, $\mathbf V\sim N(\mathbf 0,\boldsymbol\Sigma)$. Steps: draw $D$ vectors $\mathbf V^{(d)}\sim N(\mathbf 0,\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma})$ via a Cholesky factor; compute $M^{(d)}=\max_h|V^{(d)}_h|/\hat\sigma_h$; $c_{1-\alpha}$ is the empirical $1-\alpha$ quantile. Checks: 12-horizon Figure 3 block, $D=2{,}100$ (the authors' `drawnorm` on the 150×14 pseudo-panel, `pctile nq(20)`): 2.738; $D=10^6$: 2.713; $D=10^5$ in Mata (shelter, 49 horizons): 2.584 and, on a second seed path, 2.653 versus 2.679 for the housing-controls variant — the quantile's MC standard error at $D=10^5$ is about 0.01. Also verify $c_{1-\alpha}\ge z_{1-\alpha/2}$ (equality iff all correlations are one) and $c\le$ Šidák (equality iff independence).

**D9. Newey–West small-sample factor.** `newey` reports $\hat V\times n/(n-k)$; the course routine reports $\hat{\mathbf S}/(T\hat q^2)$ without it. Check: ratio 1.1124512–1.1124514 across 49 horizons, $\sqrt{323/261}=1.1124512$ (`supt_shelter2.log`). The lab project's routine takes an option to apply the factor so that pointwise s.e.'s match `newey` to $10^{-6}$.

## 9. Slides arc

1. **Title.** Inference about the response path — one covariance matrix, six claims.
2. **The referee's two sentences.** IJK's "almost uniformly negative each month" over bands that contain zero; JT's "rises to 1¼ percent by year two" over 13 stars. Which sentence does the evidence support?
3. **Three claims, three nulls.** "At month 6", "at every month in $\mathcal H$", "somewhere in $\mathcal H$" as $H_0$'s; the table of procedures; what each needs from $\boldsymbol\Sigma$.
4. **Two horizons share a shock.** $u_{t,1}=\theta_0s_{t+1}+v_{t+1}+\rho v_t$; the correlation $\rho/\sqrt{2+\rho^2}$; the corner picture (`fig-l06-two-horizons`).
5. **The stacked vector and its covariance.** $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\sim N(\boldsymbol\beta,\boldsymbol\Sigma)$; the heatmap of the unemployment path's 49×49 correlations.
6. **How to get $\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}$.** Partial out, stack the scores, one HAC: $\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}=\hat{\mathbf S}/(T\hat q^2)$; equals `gmm … vce(hac nw m)` to $10^{-8}$.
7. **Multiplicity.** Joint coverage of 13 pointwise bands: 0.51 independent, 0.69 at correlation 0.8; the curve in `fig-l06-multiplicity`.
8. **The sup-$t$ recipe.** Draw, standardize, take the max, take the quantile; $1.96\le c_{1-\alpha}\le$ Šidák $\le$ Bonferroni; the shelter bands on one plot.
9. **The Wald test and what it does not say.** $W_{0:12}=54.9$ on a path where no interval excludes zero; "somewhere" is not "where"; the condition number warning; the average over a window as the interpretable alternative (unemployment: 0.92 ± 0.39).
10. **Overlap is not a test.** $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta^Y_h-\hat\beta^X_h)$ has three terms; tenant versus owner rent.
11. **Adding up.** $\operatorname{Var}(\hat B_2)$: 0.0173 with the covariances, 0.0083 without; the long-difference LP does it in one line.
12. **A different null: significance bands.** Variance under "no response at all"; the autocorrelogram's $\pm1.96/\sqrt n$ as the special case; the shelter significance band beside its confidence band.
13. **Verdicts.** The known economy (both bands cover; pointwise stars through $h=8$, sup-$t$ through 5); shelter (3 stars, 0 sup-$t$, Wald rejects); unemployment (13 stars, 0 sup-$t$, Wald rejects, average positive). Three sentences each.
14. **Lab, practicum, and the question for Lecture 7.** Claims About a Curve; REP06/STA06; why can an LP and a VAR fitted to the same data give different paths?
